Methodological Design: A Systematic Review of Algorithmic Dual-Use in Public Policy and Warfare
Conceptual Hypothesis: Information Goods and the Frictionless Dual-Use of Algorithms
Before detailing the mechanical parameters of this study’s systematic literature review (SLR), it is necessary to articulate the tentative conceptual hypothesis that motivates the underlying research question. Expanding upon Kobi Michael’s (2008) assessment of "epistemic authority," this research posits that the mechanisms of state and military control have undergone a profound techno-discursive shift. As a theoretical heuristic, this shift can be understood through the transition from Michel Foucault’s (1975) "disciplinary societies"—characterized by physical architectures of enclosure and distinct boundaries—to Gilles Deleuze’s (1990) "societies of control," wherein power operates through continuous algorithmic modulation, digital tracking, and decentralized data networks. However, this philosophical framing serves merely as a tentative hypothesis with which to interrogate the literature, establishing the premise that algorithmicization in contemporary urban policymaking and spatial planning possesses a uniquely aggressive, inherent "dual-use" capacity.

Crucially, the dual-use nature of spatial algorithms and urban analytics must be distinguished from the historical dual-use of physical infrastructure. While traditional built environments—such as roads, railways, ports, and gas terminals—have always served both civilian commerce and military logistics, their misappropriation is constrained by physical friction, geography, and the material costs of redirecting hard assets. In contrast, the algorithmicization of policy relies entirely on software and data. To understand why algorithmic dual-use is vastly more pervasive, this research integrates Kenneth Arrow’s economic conceptualization of the "information good" (Arrow, 1962). Arrow established that information—unlike physical commodities—is essentially non-rivalrous and possesses a near-zero marginal cost of reproduction and distribution. When a municipal government deploys "Smart City" sensor architectures, geographic information systems (GIS), or demographic pattern-recognition software, it is generating an information good. Because software code and data lakes are infinitely replicable at zero marginal cost, the civilian tools used to optimize traffic flows, apportion housing, or manage public health can be instantaneously and frictionlessly retrofitted for military intelligence, targeting grids, or the spatial unmaking of the private dwelling (King, 2004). This research hypothesizes that the frictionless scalability of information goods fundamentally dissolves the barrier between civilian urban management and military spatial dominance, a convergence that will be empirically tested by tracking the academic and policy discourse over the last thirty-five years.

Justification for a Systematic Review Methodology
To rigorously investigate whether and how this shift toward algorithmic dual-use has been recognized, debated, and conceptualized within academia and the policy sphere, this dissertation will employ a Systematic Literature Review. As articulated in the Urban Studies Research: Design, Methods & Ethics module (URBAN5119), contemporary public policy and social science research have broadly experienced an "evidence turn," moving away from traditional narrative literature reviews (Clark et al., 2021; Tranfield et al., 2003). Traditional narrative reviews, while foundational for philosophical exploration, are highly susceptible to selection bias, allowing a researcher to cherry-pick texts that solely confirm predefined theoretical inclinations—a significant risk when operating with provocative frameworks like Deleuze’s (1990) control societies or De Landa’s (1992, 2014) machinic state spaces. Therefore, to ensure that the Foucault/Deleuze framework remains an interrogated hypothesis rather than an unearned conclusion, the SLR methodology is adopted for its transparency, replicability, and auditable data trail. Rather than utilizing the SLR to identify "what works" in an administrative policy context, this methodology is adapted here to systematically map a macro-level discursive and epistemological shift across disparate fields (urban studies, computer science, public administration, and defense studies), effectively treating the academic and grey literature as the primary dataset.

Search Strategy, Timeline, and Identifying Relevant Works
The parameters of this systematic review are designed to capture the structural evolution of digital infrastructure and policy discourse from 1990 to 2025. This longitudinal thirty-five-year timeframe is critical, as it allows the review to index the distinct developmental epochs of digitalization: the advent of the early civilian internet and initial physical digital infrastructure build-outs (1990s); the rise of Web 2.0, platform capitalism, and mass data harvesting (2000s); the transition into Web 3.0 and the integration of IoT (Internet of Things) into urban planning (2010s); and finally, the ongoing Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR), characterized by ubiquitous artificial intelligence, machine learning, and algorithmic targeting in contemporary conflicts (such as those in Gaza and Ukraine).

To ensure a comprehensive capture of relevant literature, the database search strategy will be dual-pronged, indexing both peer-reviewed academic literature and high-level grey literature. For peer-reviewed journal articles, the research will utilize Web of Science and Scopus through complex Boolean search strings intersecting key conceptual domains: (algorithmic governance OR urban analytics OR smart city) AND (dual-use OR militarization OR targeting OR zero marginal cost) AND (spatial planning OR urban policy OR domicide). However, because technological development in software and military AI consistently outpaces the slow cycle of academic peer review, indexing grey literature is absolutely paramount to capturing real-time shifts in policy and military doctrine. Therefore, alongside academic repositories, this review will systematically query leading policy and government databases. [Note to self/suggested repositories]: These will include Overton (an exhaustive database of global policy documents, parliamentary transcripts, and think-tank outputs), Policy Commons, the OECD iLibrary for international governance frameworks, and defense-specific think tanks such as the RAND Corporation and the Defense Technical Information Center (DTIC) to capture the defense sector’s conceptualization of urban analytics.

Data Extraction, Thematic Coding, and Synthesis
Following the identification of the literature, the corpus will be filtered utilizing a modified PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flow diagram, applying strict inclusion and exclusion criteria based on relevance to the intersection of algorithms, urban space, and dual-use policy. Excluded literature will primarily consist of purely technical, computational computer science papers that lack a verifiable link to spatial planning, public policy, or sociological impacts. The remaining robust dataset will then be imported into a qualitative data analysis software tool (such as NVivo or ATLAS.ti) to facilitate rigorous coding.

Crucially, the thematic framework utilized in this coding process remains strictly tentative and iterative. While the study enters the coding phase with a priori conceptual nodes—derived from Kobi Michael’s "epistemic authority," Kenneth Arrow’s "information goods," Manuel De Landa’s "machinic state space," and Peter King’s (2004) phenomenological "private dwelling"—these are points of departure rather than fixed rigidities. The review will employ a hybrid inductive-deductive coding strategy. This means that while we will deliberately search for discussions regarding the weaponization of civilian algorithmic planning, we will simultaneously allow new, unanticipated themes to emerge organically from the parsed literature. Through this systematic charting and synthesis, the ensuing data chapter will empirically demonstrate whether the literature confirms the hypothesis: that the zero-marginal-cost nature of digital infrastructure has resulted in a seamless, unified algorithmic architecture where public policy and spatial destruction operate using identical tools.
