# Tactical Dominance, and the Substitution of Strategy in the Data Era

In the article, _"The Israel Defense Forces as an epistemic authority,"_ Kobi Michael identifies a profound breakdown in the civil-military "discourse space" in Israel during the era of Low Intensity Conflict (LIC). He argues that the political echelon, crippled by "strategic helplessness" (cf. Michael, 2017) and operating with "creative fuzziness," effectively abdicated its responsibility to chart a political strategy. Into this void stepped the Israel Defense Forces (IDF), claiming the role of an "epistemic authority" e.g the absolute arbiter of knowledge and truth. In 2008, this authority was largely conceptual and linguistic, operationalised through systematic staff work and the military’s Estimation of the Situation (EOS).

However, the catastrophic deterioration of the conflict—from 2014 to the current systemic destruction of Gaza beginning in 2023—demands an urgent update to Michael’s framework. Today, the IDF’s epistemic authority is has become intensely algorithmic.  Within the context of Israel as a modern garrison state, the IDF asserts this epistemic dominance through urban data and surveillance technology. This authority facilitates a dangerous illusion in Israeli (and wider) policymaking: the masking of a profound lack of political strategy with data-driven, tactical dominance. This displacement of strategy by data is evident not only in the deliberate spatial unmaking of Gaza, but also in the wider, ongoing US-Israeli war against Iran.

Michael (2008) utilises Foucault’s power/knowledge dynamic to explain how the IDF functioned as a "truth and lie agent" (ibid. p. 444). Since 2008, the mechanisms of truth-making have evolved. The traditional military EOS has been superseded by AI-driven target generation systems, Geographic Information Systems (GIS), and wide-area urban surveillance capabilities (see [gorgon stare](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mJLr0KMsRAA)). The military maintains its epistemic hegemony through the God’s Eye View (GEV) (cf. Sadowski, 2026) of remote sensing.

As I argued in my [recent essay on the limits of data](https://www.densetheory.cc/dense-theory/systemic-destruction-and-the-limits-of-data/), the advent of Big Data ushered in the dangerous positivist assumption of the 'end' of theory; that sufficient data volume makes theoretical frameworks obsolete. Under this assumption, civilian politicians are easily seduced by the seeming objectivity of the military’s data points. If the IDF of 2008 used complex linguistic knowledge maps to dominate the political discourse space, the IDF of 2024 does so through its supreme command of surveillance technology. To challenge this epistemic monopoly, a powerful counter-dynamic has emerged: independent initiatives like Forensic Architecture and Gaza Maps(<https://gazamaps.com>) now engage in "counter-forensics". In doing so, they aim to inject International Humanitarian Law (IHL) back into the dataset, and to prove that data cannot actually speak for itself. This is the difference between urban management in conflcit and systemic urbicide.

Michael notes that in the absence of civilian knowledge centers, Cabinet discussions invariably revert to the tactical and operational level, "lacking sufficient depth" (Michel 2008 p. 439). Today, this reliance on tactic-over-strategy has been supercharged by urban analytics. AI and surveillance technologies are inherently optimising mechanisms (cf. techno-solutionism); they are designed to solve tactical problems (e.g., locating a target, dividing a population, clearing a route). They cannot, however, arrive to a political endgame. In the case of Gaza, the spatial analytics documented by Forensic Architecture—the clearing of 36% of the territory, the engineering of the Netzarim Corridor, the systematic implementation of "evacuation grids"—represent an unprecedented level of tactical urban dominance. Yet, it exposes an strategic vacuum. The "Evacuation Grid" functions as a municipal zoning tool weaponised for mass displacement (_domicide_), but it answers no long-term strategic questions about post-combat governance or political resolution.

This critique of tactical supremacy substituting for strategy could be extended to the ongoing US-Israeli war against Iran. The operations characterising this shadow war—precision assassinations, sophisticated cyber warfare, algorithmic intelligence gathering, and supply-line disruption (cf. Israel's pager attacks against Hizzbollah)—are masterclasses in technological and tactical dominance. Yet, just as in the Palestinian theatre these data-driven strikes exist in a state of political "fuzziness." Tactical disruption loop algorithms (cf. Stuxnet) are endlessly executed to delay Iranian nuclear capability and neutralise proxies, but they fail to coalesce into a coherent geopolitical strategy.

### Implications for Policymaking and the Urban Environment

What are the implications of this technological evolution for policymaking? When war zones become the testing ground for urban sensor networks and AI, the nature of civilian oversight breaks down.

First, the 'algorithmicization' of the military’s epistemic authority heavily degrades democratic control. Politicians, intimidated by the velocity and volume of military big data, effectively rubber-stamp operations. The aesthetic of "Smart City" data—translated into target queues and structural damage assessments—sanitises violent urban management, absolving policymakers of moral and strategic responsibility. This is the dark mirror of *Surveillance Capitalism* (cf. Shoshanna Zubuff book of the same title or most recently Y. Varoufakis Technofeudalism). Where commercial big data extracts a *behavioural surplus* from urban populations to optimise profit, military big data extracts an *intelligence surplus* to optimise spatial control and _home unmaking_.

Second, technology is inherently dual-use. The same GIS infrastructure, computer vision, and network analysis that policymakers endorse for peacetime urban management are seamlessly retrofitted for use in a conflict zone. Policymakers must realise that when they allow a military establishment to possess total authority over urban data, they are surrendering the right to govern the city itself.

### Conclusion

Kobi Michael’s assertion that the IDF acts as an epistemic authority remains profoundly accurate, but the tools of that authority have radically changed. By replacing the intellectual encounter of civil-military relations with the automated output of AI and mass surveillance, the state has achieved total tactical dominance at the expense of strategic viability. Addressing this in policy requires piercing the black box of military data analytics, returning the focus of the "discourse space" from the optimization of tactical manoeuvres to the political and humanitarian realities of the urban and geopolitical spheres.
