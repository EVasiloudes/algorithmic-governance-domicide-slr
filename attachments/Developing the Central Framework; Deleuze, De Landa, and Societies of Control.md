# Developing the Central Framework; Deleuze, De Landa, and Societies of Control
### Developing the Central Framework: Deleuze, De Landa, and Societies of Control

Kobi Michael’s (2008) assessment of the IDF’s "epistemic authority" provides a vital foundation for understanding how militaries dominate civil-political discourse. However, Michael anchors his analysis in a broadly Foucauldian paradigm of power/knowledge, which is implicitly tied to Foucault’s concept of *disciplinary societies*. Disciplinary power operates through vast spaces of enclosure—the prison, the barracks, the factory, the hospital—seeking to concentrate, distribute in space, and order in time (Foucault, 1975). Yet, to understand the deeply algorithmic nature of contemporary public policy, urban management, and modern warfare, we must transition our theoretical framing to align with the technological reality of the 21st century.

Gilles Deleuze’s (1990) *Postscript on the Societies of Control* provides the necessary theoretical evolution. Deleuze argues that the environments of enclosure described by Foucault are universally in crisis. We have moved from a society of *molds* (disciplinary enclosures) to a society of *modulation*, wherein control operates like a "self-deforming cast that will continuously change from one moment to the other" (Deleuze, 1990, p. 4). In a society of control, the locus of power is not the physical boundary, but the algorithm, the password, and the continuous tracking of data. Under this regime, individuals are no longer treated as unified subjects; they become "dividuals"—banks of data, samples, and targetable spatial coordinates.

This theoretical shift is crucial for examining the algorithmicization of policymaking and warfare. The modern military or state rather modulates control via continuous, free-floating surveillance networks. Manuel De Landa expands upon this by examining the transition toward "machinic assemblages" in human governance and warfare (De Landa, 1992). Drawing on the complex dynamics of state spaces (De Landa, 2014), the algorithmic tools utilized by the state treat urban populations as dynamic fields to be mapped, predicted, and optimized.

Importantly, this regime of control is not isolated to the battlefield—it extends inextricably into the civilian logic of urban planning and housing. To interrogate this cross-axis, this research integrates Peter King’s (2004) conceptualization of the *Private Dwelling*. For King, housing is fundamentally defined by the concept of privacy, boundary, and the phenomenological experience of dwelling. In a disciplinary society, the home is a definitive interior space. However, in the algorithmic garrison state (tech garrison state?), the private dwelling is systematically penetrated, mapped by GIS, Here, home-unmaking and algorithmic governance intersect requires a rigorous charting of how global academic discourse has shifted in response to these technologies.

### Methodological Approach: A Systematic Review of Discursive Shifts

To empirically investigate this theoretical transition—from disciplinary institutional authority to algorithmic control in both public policy and military strategy—I deploy employ a Systematic Literature Review (SLR).

There is a growing demand in modern academia to move beyond the traditional "narrative review." Narrative reviews, while vital for intellectual craftsmanship, often lack a transparent audit trail, allowing for selection bias. The contemporary public policy landscape has undergone an "evidence turn," increasingly prioritizing reproducible methodologies to identify "what works" (==CITE CITE==). While systematic reviews are typically deployed in the social sciences to evaluate the effectiveness of specific policy interventions (such as housing provisions or health interventions), I will adapt this highly structured methodology to conduct a deeply critical, theoretical investigation. Instead of searching for "what works," this systematic review will rigorously map a *discursive shift*: how the academic literature across urban studies, public policy, and military studies has increasingly conceptualized algorithms, big data, and surveillance as central tenets of state control.

By adopting the parameters of a systematic review, this research will leverage secondary data to generate unbiased, comprehensive accounts of the literature, avoiding the accusation of cherry-picking sources to support a pre-existing Deleuzian critique. This methodology aligns with the necessity to create a replicable, scientific, and transparent process (Tranfield et al., 2003).

### Research Parameters and the PRISMA Framework

This research will structure its data collection around the five core steps of a systematic review, utilizing a modified PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) framework:

**1. Framing the Question:**
 The central research questions driving the review are: *How does the literature conceptualize the shift toward algorithmic governance in both civilian urban policymaking and military operations?* and *In what ways does the literature address the dual-use nature of spatial analytics in compromising the "private dwelling" (King, 2004)?*

**2. Identifying Relevant Work:**
 The data extraction will rely on secondary data gathered from Web of Science, Scopus, and relevant policy repositories (==should we not include grey lit?==). Boolean search strings will be constructed to capture the intersection of these themes. Keywords will include combinations of: `("algorithmic policy" OR "urban analytics" OR "smart city" OR "surveillance capitalism") AND ("military operations" OR "targeting" OR "OSINT" OR "dual-use") AND ("housing" OR "domicide" OR "urban planning")`.

**3. Assessing Quality and Applying Criteria:**
 The PRISMA flow diagram will be utilized to filter records.

- *Inclusion criteria:* Peer-reviewed journal articles, prominent public policy white-papers, and critical theoretical texts published from 2010 to present (capturing the primary boom of Big Data and AI implementation).
- *Exclusion criteria:* Literature focusing purely on the technical/computational mechanics of software engineering without reference to public policy, governance, urban space, or defense.

**4. Summarizing the Evidence:**
 A "Systematic Review Map" will be created. Rather than a meta-analysis of quantitative statistics, this map will categorize the literature conceptually. Articles will be coded across three primary axes:

- *Epistemic Authority:* Literature focusing on the black-boxing of decision-making by AI.
- *The Machinic City:* Literature treating urban populations as managed data fields (as per De Landa, 2014).
- *Spatial Collapse/Domicide:* Literature addressing the weaponization of urban planning and the infringement upon the private dwelling (King, 2004).

**5. Interpreting the Findings:**
 The final synthesis of the systematic review will bridge the theoretical and the empirical. By critically evaluating the mapped literature through Deleuze’s "Society of Control," this dissertation will provide a robust structural critique of how data technology is homogenizing the tools of civilian governance and military destruction. This systematic interpretation will form the substantive data chapter of the dissertation, culminating in a clearly articulated agenda for future research into public policy frameworks capable of resisting algorithmic urban dominance.
