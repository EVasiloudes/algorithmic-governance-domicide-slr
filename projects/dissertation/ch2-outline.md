## §2 — Arrow: Information Goods and Structural Dual-Use (~400 words)

### Argument
Kenneth Arrow's 1962 analysis of information as an economic good identifies three defining properties:
1. **Non-rivalrous** — use by one actor does not deplete it
2. **Near-zero marginal cost of reproduction** — once produced, copying is essentially free
3. **Frictionless transfer** across domains — an algorithm developed for one purpose requires minimal adaptation for another

This is the structural basis of the dual-use claim. A GIS routing algorithm built for waste collection is not *reinterpreted* as a targeting tool — it is *copied* and *reparameterised*. The economic friction that limits dual-use in physical infrastructure (a bridge cannot be lifted and dropped in a war zone) simply does not exist for software.

### Key moves
- Contrast with *physical* dual-use (e.g., trucks used for military logistics) — that requires retrofitting, retraining, redeployment. Software dual-use requires none of these.
- Introduce the *marginal cost differential* as the key mechanism: it makes algorithmic dual-use not an edge case but a structural tendency.
- Flag that this is often implicit or absent from the dual-use literature in urban studies — a gap the SLR will test.

### Sources
- Arrow, K., 1962. Economic Welfare and the Allocation of Resources for Invention
- **Optional:** Stiglitz / Greenwald on knowledge as a public good (not essential, word budget is tight)

### Trim note
If word count is squeezed, §2 can be folded into §1 (~100 words of economic premise). It's foundational but not where the analytical weight sits.

### Bridge → §3
Arrow explains *that* software dual-use is cheap and easy. Deleuze explains *why the institutional environment is uniquely receptive* to it.

## §3 — Deleuze: From Discipline to Control (~500 words)

### Argument
Deleuze's 1990 *Postscript on the Societies of Control* describes a rupture in the logic of power:
- **Disciplinary societies** (Foucault) operated through spaces of enclosure — the prison, the barracks, the factory, the school. Power was visible, spatial, and punctual.
- **Control societies** operate through *continuous modulation* — the password, the database, the algorithm. Power is ambient, invisible, and always-on.

This is the institutional corollary of Arrow's economics. Disciplinary institutions had physical and procedural inertia — changing a prison regime was expensive and contested. Control modulation has no such inertia. The same urban sensor network can optimise traffic flow *and* enforce movement restrictions, because both are just parameter changes in the same algorithmic system.

The key concept for this chapter: **modulation.** Deleuze describes control as "a self-deforming cast that will continuously change from one moment to the other." This is the precise mechanism that makes dual-use architectural rather than accidental.

### Key moves
- Establish the discipline → control shift as the *diagnostic* frame for reading the SLR findings in Chapter 5
- Introduce the **dividual** concept — the individual decomposed into data points — as the bridge to De Landa's targetable city
- **Crucially:** this is the section that anchors the transition from visible institutions to ambient modulation — which is why algorithmic dual-use is harder to regulate than physical dual-use (regulation targets visible boundaries, not continuous flows)

### Sources
- Deleuze, G., 1990. Postscript on the Societies of Control
- Foucault, M., 1975. Discipline and Punish (background, minimal citation)

### Bridge → §4
If control societies modulate through data, then cities — the most data-saturated environments — become the primary terrain. De Landa provides the vocabulary for this.

## §4 — De Landa: Machinic Assemblages and the Targetable City (~450 words)

### Argument
Manuel De Landa extends Deleuze's framework into urban and military domains through the concept of the **machinic assemblage** — a heterogeneous network of human and non-human components that functions as a directed system.

Two key texts:
- *War in the Age of Intelligent Machines* (1992) — traces the automation of military decision-making and the emergence of the battle-space as a programmable field
- *Intensive Science and Virtual Philosophy* (2014) — develops the ontology of intensive quantities and population-level dynamics

The critical move for this dissertation: De Landa's framework enables us to see the *city* not as a collection of buildings and people, but as a **targetable field** — a space where the same population-level data (density, flow, infrastructure networks) functions as both an *optimisation input* for civilian governance and a *targeting matrix* for military destruction.

This is the spatial analogue of Arrow's economic dual-use and Deleuze's institutional modulation. The city becomes a **singular machine** parameterised for either welfare or warfare — and nothing in the architecture of the data infrastructure distinguishes between the two.

### Key moves
- Establish the city-as-assemblage as a *unit of analysis* for the SLR coding
- Link to the **machinic city** coding theme in Chapter 4
- Show how De Landa bridges Deleuze (abstract logic of control) and Michael/Kemp/Weizman (empirical cases of destruction)

### Sources
- De Landa, M., 1992. War in the Age of Intelligent Machines
- De Landa, M., 2014. Intensive Science and Virtual Philosophy

### Trim note
If tight, the "intensive science" ontology can be reduced to a footnote. The *War* material is more directly useful for the dual-use argument.

### Bridge → §5
De Landa shows us *how* the city becomes a programmed field. Michael shows us *who gets to program it* — and why that epistemic authority is structurally dangerous.

## §5 — Michael: Epistemic Authority Through Data (~450 words)

### Argument
Kobi Michael's 2008 concept of the IDF as an **epistemic authority** — the arbiter of what counts as true knowledge within the civil-military discourse space — is the most directly applicable framework for the dual-use claim.

Michael's original argument (developed in the context of the 2006 Lebanon war and the Second Intifada):
- The political echelon, experiencing "strategic helplessness," abdicated strategic direction
- The IDF filled this vacuum by claiming epistemic authority — not just operational capability, but *the right to define reality*
- This authority was exercised through linguistic and institutional means: systematic staff work, the military's Estimation of the Situation (EOS), and the production of privileged knowledge

This dissertation **updates** Michael's framework in three ways:
1. **Algorithmic turn:** Epistemic authority is now exercised through data analytics, AI targeting systems, and GIS — not just linguistic dominance. The authority is harder to contest because it appears objective and computational.
2. **Material upgrade:** Drawing on Sadowski (2026), the "God's Eye View" of remote sensing gives the military an epistemic monopoly on spatial truth — only the military has the sensor network.
3. **Collateral penetration:** The same sensor networks that produce civilian urban data (traffic, zoning, population density) seamlessly feed into military intelligence — the dual-use convergence is epistemic before it is operational.

### Key moves
- This is the section that makes the dual-use claim *political*, not just technical
- Connect to the **epistemic authority & black-boxing** coding theme in Chapter 4
- Establish the Gaza case as the empirical limit-case — where epistemic authority produces not just policy distortion but domicide

### Sources
- Michael, K., 2007. The IDF as an epistemic authority. *Journal of Strategic Studies*
- Sadowski, J., 2026. Machine's Eye View. *Science, Technology, & Human Values*
- Optional: Arendt on truth and politics (for nuance — only if words permit)

### Trim note
This section should not be compressed. It is the political heart of the framework. If savings are needed, take them from §2 or §6.

### Bridge → §6
Michael describes the mechanism of control. King, Graham, and Weizman describe what happens when that control reaches its spatial conclusion.

## §6 — King / Graham / Weizman: Domicide, Urbicide, Vertical Sovereignty (~500 words)

### Argument
This section assembles three thinkers who describe the terminus of the framework — spatial destruction as a deliberate outcome of algorithmic governance.

**Peter King** (2004, *Private Dwelling*):
- The home as a phenomenologically defined interior space — privacy, boundary, dwelling
- Algorithmic penetration of the home (GIS, predictive policing, smart city sensors) is not just surveillance but an *ontological* violation of dwelling
- Dual-use implication: the same data that optimises housing allocation can identify homes for demolition

**Stephen Graham** (2006, *Cities, War, and Terrorism*; 2011, *Cities Under Siege*):
- The "new military urbanism" — cities as the primary battlespace, urban infrastructure as military target
- Vertical sovereignty (drones, aerial surveillance) changes the geometry of urban conflict
- Civilian urban management systems are co-opted for military operations — the dual-use claim is not theoretical but empirically documented

**Eyal Weizman** (2007, *Hollow Land*):
- The Israeli occupation as a laboratory for vertical sovereignty and datafied spatial control
- Forensic Architecture's documentation of Gaza (2023–present) as the limit-case of algorithmic domicide
- The "evacuation grid" — a municipal zoning tool (GIS grid system) weaponised for mass displacement

### Key moves
- Frame King's *private dwelling* as the *victim* of the framework — what is destroyed when Arrow + Deleuze + De Landa + Michael converge
- Graham and Weizman provide the *empirical anchors* that the SLR will test more broadly
- Introduce the **spatial collapse & home unmaking** coding theme in Chapter 4
- **Crucially:** this section must show that the framework is not abstract — it explains real outcomes

### Sources
- King, P., 2004. Private Dwelling
- Graham, S., 2006. Cities, War, and Terrorism
- Graham, S., 2011. Cities Under Siege
- Weizman, E., 2007. Hollow Land
- Forensic Architecture, 2023–present. Various reports

### Bridge → §7
Each section of this chapter has introduced a concept and a corresponding coding theme. The final section shows how they fit together as a *testable analytical framework* for the SLR.

## Writing notes

### Tense & citation style
- Present tense for theoretical claims that are still active: "Deleuze *argues*" / "Arrow *identifies*"
- Past tense for historical context: "Foucault *located* disciplinary societies in the 18th and 19th centuries"
- Citation: author-date throughout, consistent with the rest of the dissertation
- Use square-bracketed `[VERIFY]` flags for any source the agent hasn't personally verified

### Section ordering (not negotiable)
The order is not arbitrary. It follows a logical dependency chain:
- Arrow first because he's the *foundation* (material economic premise)
- Deleuze second because he explains the *institutional receptivity* (social premise)
- De Landa third because he locates both in *urban space* (spatial premise)
- Michael fourth because he's the *mechanism* (political premise)
- King/Graham/Weizman fifth because they're the *evidence* (empirical terminus)

Jumping sections would confuse this dependency. If words are tight, compress within sections, don't reorder.

### Dual-use: defining it explicitly
Early in §1 (or §2), define "dual-use" clearly for the purposes of this dissertation:
> Dual-use here means: *a technology developed for civilian urban governance that is structurally transferable to military urban destruction with minimal modification, where the transfer is facilitated by the economic properties of software, the institutional logic of control societies, and the epistemic authority of algorithmic systems.*

This prevents the reader from confusing this with generic technology dual-use (GPS, drones, etc.).

### What this chapter does not do
- Does **not** survey the empirical literature on smart cities, surveillance, or urban warfare (that's Chapter 4)
- Does **not** describe the methodology (that's Chapter 3)
- Does **not** evaluate or critique the sources in depth (that's Chapters 4–5)
- Does **not** introduce novel theory — it assembles, contextualises, and operationalises existing concepts
